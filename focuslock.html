FocusLock

A native Android focus app that enforces distraction-free work sessions through deep OS integration.

01

The problem

Most “focus” and app-blocker tools fail at the exact moment they’re needed: the instant you feel the urge to quit, you swipe the app away or uninstall it in seconds. I wanted to explore how far a productivity app could go in surviving that moment of weakness — using legitimate Android system APIs — while still respecting the user’s control over their own device.

target userSomeone trying to break compulsive phone habits who wants self-imposed friction stronger than a simple “are you sure?” dialog.

02

What I built

FocusLock lets a user pick distracting apps, start a timed focus session, and have those apps actively blocked for the duration. The session is engineered to persist through the ways people normally escape:

  • App blocking via an AccessibilityService that detects the foreground app and redirects away from blocked ones to a custom “blocked” screen.
  • A persistent countdown running in a foreground service with an ongoing notification.
  • Reboot survival — a BootReceiver restores the active session and its remaining time after a restart, so powering off isn’t an escape hatch.
  • Offline home geofencing — using raw GNSS (LocationManager.GPS_PROVIDER), focus mode can auto-trigger when the user arrives home, with zero internet or Wi-Fi dependency.
  • Scheduled sessions and lifetime statistics, persisted in SQLite.
03

Technical highlights

01

Clean, testable architecture

MVVM + Repository pattern separates the Compose UI from domain logic and data sources, making the service-heavy codebase manageable.

02

Coordinating Android background components

The hardest part wasn’t any single API — it was making the AccessibilityService, foreground service, AlarmManager, and boot receiver agree on one source of truth for session state across process death and reboots.

03

Modern Android constraints

Targeting Android 14 meant working within foreground-service type rules, scoped storage, and runtime-permission gating for notifications and location.

04

Material 3 dark UI

Built entirely in Jetpack Compose.

04

The decision I’m most proud of

While hardening the app against being quit, I hit a real engineering-ethics fork: I could use the Device Admin / Device Owner APIs to make the app effectively impossible to uninstall. I deliberately chose not to.

An app the device owner can’t remove — and that silences the system’s own safety warnings — is indistinguishable from malware, both to Google Play Protect and to the person holding the phone. Worse, a bug in that path can brick a device into a factory-reset-only state (I actually reproduced this failure mode during testing).

So I redesigned the protection around a principle:

impossible on impulse, always possible with real effort.

Removal requires a cooldown, a deliberate confirmation, and an escalating penalty — strong enough to defeat a 10-second urge, but never a trap. This turned out to be a better product decision too: a phone someone can’t escape gets factory-reset in frustration; friction they chose for themselves is one they keep.

05

What I learned

  • How Android’s accessibility, foreground-service, alarm, and boot subsystems actually interact under process death and reboot.
  • Designing persistent state that survives the OS killing your process.
  • That “make it unbreakable” is often the wrong requirement — the right one is “make it resilient without removing the user’s control.”
  • Security and distribution realities: signing config hygiene, Play Protect’s behavioral heuristics, and sideload distribution via a hosted landing page.
06

Outcome

A fully functional Android app with a Compose UI, reboot-persistent enforcement engine, offline geofencing, and a self-hosted web landing page for distribution — plus a clear-eyed view of where self-control tooling should stop.
